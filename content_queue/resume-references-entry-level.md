# References on an Entry-Level Resume: When and How to List Them

Most entry-level candidates do not need to put references directly on the resume. A separate, well-prepared list is usually more useful. It protects your contacts' information, saves space for projects, and lets you share the right references when an employer asks.

## Who this page is for

This guide is for you if:

- you are applying for internships or a first professional role
- you are unsure whether to write “references available upon request”
- you need academic, volunteer, freelance, or part-time references
- you want a professional format for a separate reference list

The examples below are illustrative. Always ask permission before sharing someone's details.

## Do not use resume space by default

Unless the job posting asks for references on the resume, leave them off. Also remove the generic line “references available upon request.” The employer already knows it can ask.

This approach gives you more room for education, projects, and relevant skills. It also prevents you from sending every contact's phone number and email address to every employer.

A separate list lets you select references for the role and provide them at the appropriate stage.

## Choose people who can describe your work

A useful reference can discuss your responsibilities, reliability, and working behavior from direct experience.

Good entry-level choices include:

- A professor or academic adviser who supervised a substantial project.
- An internship, part-time, or volunteer supervisor.
- A freelance client or project stakeholder.
- A research mentor, club adviser, or coach who worked with you closely.
- A teammate who can describe collaboration, though a supervisor is usually stronger.

Avoid family members and friends who cannot give a professional assessment. A well-known professor who barely knows you is usually less useful than a supervisor who can describe your work in detail.

## Ask before adding someone to the list

Contact each person before sharing their information. Explain the role you are applying for, why you are asking, and what parts of your work they may be asked about.

A simple request can be:

- I am applying for entry-level software roles and wondered whether you would be comfortable serving as a reference for my work on the scheduling project. I can share the resume and a short summary of my contributions.

Give them time to decline. If they hesitate, choose someone else. A reluctant or unprepared reference is not ideal.

Send the current resume, the project link, and a short reminder of your shared work when they agree.

## Prepare a separate reference list

Use the same name and basic contact information as your resume so the document is easy to associate with your application.

A simple entry includes:

- Reference name
- Professional title and organization
- Relationship to you
- Professional email and phone number, with permission
- Optional project or context that helps the employer

Example:

**Jordan Smith**  
Professor of Computer Science, University Name  
Academic adviser for the course-scheduling project  
jordan.smith@example.edu | (555) 010-2030

**Morgan Lee**  
Volunteer Coordinator, Community Library  
Supervisor for weekly digital-skills workshops  
morgan.lee@example.org | (555) 010-2040

Use the contact information the reference prefers. Do not publish the list on a public portfolio.

## Match references to the application

Different people can support different parts of your background.

For a software role, a professor who reviewed your code and a supervisor who saw your reliability may be a strong pair. For a data internship, a statistics professor and a volunteer reporting supervisor may offer better evidence.

Two or three prepared references are usually enough. More contacts do not necessarily add better information. Choose people who can give specific examples rather than general praise.

Keep a short private note about each reference's preferred contact method, time zone, and the projects they know best.

## Handle academic and project references well

A professor should do more than confirm that you attended class. Brief them on the project you want to discuss: the problem, your individual contribution, validation, and outcome.

For group projects, make sure the reference can identify your component accurately. For independent projects, share a clean repository or report so they can review it before speaking with an employer.

If a professor cannot serve as a reference, a project mentor, club adviser, or supervisor from another activity may still know your work well.

## Know when to send the list

Follow the application instructions. Some employers request references during the application; others ask after interviews.

If they request a separate file, use a clear name such as `Firstname-Lastname-References.pdf`. Check the contact details immediately before sending and let your references know which employer may contact them.

Do not add the list as an unexpected second page to a one-page resume. A separate attachment or form entry is easier to process.

## Common reference mistakes

- Writing “references available upon request” on every resume.
- Sharing contacts without permission.
- Choosing friends or family instead of professional contacts.
- Using a well-known person who cannot discuss your work.
- Publishing phone numbers and emails on a public website.
- Giving every employer the same generic list regardless of role.
- Failing to tell references which company may call.

## Use a simple reference request and update workflow

When you first ask, make the request easy to answer:

- Hello [Name], I am applying for [role type] and would be grateful if you would consider serving as a reference for my work on [project or responsibility]. I can send my current resume and a short summary of my contribution. Please let me know whether you are comfortable with this and which contact details you prefer me to share.

After they agree, send only useful material: the resume, one project link, and two or three reminders about your shared work. Do not ask them to read your entire portfolio.

Before an employer receives the list, send a brief update:

- Thank you again for agreeing to be a reference. [Employer] may contact you about my application for [role]. The main project I discussed is [project], especially my work on [component].

Finish the process with a thank-you message. Whether or not you receive an offer, maintaining the relationship matters more than this one application.

## Choose references for a nontraditional background

If you are self-taught, a former client, project collaborator, community organizer, or mentor can sometimes verify your work better than a professor. Choose someone who has seen you solve problems and finish responsibilities.

For a bootcamp graduate, an instructor or capstone reviewer may be a strong choice. For a career changer, a previous supervisor can describe reliability while a new project mentor can describe technical preparation. This gives the employer evidence from both stages of your transition.

Do not use a mentor only because their title sounds impressive. The person should remember your work well enough to answer specific questions about what you did and how you responded to feedback.

## Maintain the list between applications

Review your reference list every few months. Roles change, email addresses expire, and supervisors move to new organizations. Update titles and preferred contact details rather than relying on an old document.

Keep your references informed about major new projects so they can speak about your current work. A brief update is easier for them than receiving an unexpected call about a project they have never seen.

## Reconfirm availability for a new search cycle

Agreement last year does not guarantee availability today. Before starting a new round of applications, send a short message and confirm that each person is still willing and able to respond within the employer's likely timeframe.

## Final reference check

Confirm that each person has agreed to serve and is comfortable with the contact details you provide. Check the spelling of names, titles, organizations, and email addresses.

Send a short update when you share the list with an employer. Include the role and any project that may be relevant. Finally, thank your references afterward and tell them the outcome when appropriate.

## FAQ

### How many references should an entry-level candidate prepare?

Two or three well-chosen references are usually enough. Prepare a small pool so you can select people who best support each role.

### Should references go directly on the resume?

Usually no, unless the employer specifically requests them there. Use a separate list so the resume can focus on your qualifications and your contacts keep some privacy.

### Can a professor be a professional reference?

Yes, especially when they supervised a project, research, or substantial coursework and can discuss your working behavior in detail.

### Is “references available upon request” necessary?

No. It is generally unnecessary and takes space without adding information. Prepare the list and provide it when the employer asks.
